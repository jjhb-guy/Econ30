Why I got interested in poverty research

Sophomore year I took America: Unequal with Professor David Grusky and loved it. I met with him, started researching at the Stanford Center on Poverty and Inequality, and have been there ever since—most recently updating his original microclass framework while learning about CPI’s California Poverty Measure, American Opportunity Study, and trend-data infrastructure.

That work opened up a specific question: how has poverty and inequality research developed over the decades—and how does it align, or not, with real-world trends and occurrences? Below, some of the papers and projects that made the field feel so alive to me, before the data sections take over.

Deep dive

Brief Timeline of Poverty Research

Key moments in measurement, legislation, institutions, and scholarship that shaped how America understands poverty.

1907
Russell Sage Foundation established
Margaret Olivia Sage creates the nation's first general-purpose foundation with a $10 million gift, dedicated to "the improvement of social and living conditions in the United States." It becomes one of the earliest funders of social-science research on poverty. Source PDF →
1962
Michael Harrington publishes The Other America
Harrington's book exposes the "invisible poor" and helps ignite political will for federal antipoverty action. Dwight MacDonald's 1963 article amplifies the argument, and John Kenneth Galbraith's earlier work provides intellectual grounding. Source PDF →
Jan 1964
LBJ declares the "War on Poverty"
In his State of the Union address, President Lyndon Johnson announces the War on Poverty. By August the Economic Opportunity Act is signed, creating the Office of Economic Opportunity (OEO) and launching Head Start, Job Corps, Community Action Program, Legal Services, and more. First-year funding: ~$800 million ($6 billion in 2014 dollars). Source PDF →
1965
Mollie Orshansky's poverty thresholds adopted
The OEO adopts a working definition of poverty based on Orshansky's methodology: the cost of a minimum diet × 3. Medicaid and Medicare also pass this year as extensions of Social Security. Source PDF →
1966
Institute for Research on Poverty (IRP) founded
The University of Wisconsin–Madison establishes IRP as the nation's first federally funded center for poverty research, providing a model that future centers will follow. Source PDF →
1967–68
First Census poverty statistics & PSID launches
The Census Bureau publishes its first poverty estimates (1967). In 1968 the Panel Study of Income Dynamics begins tracking 4,802 families longitudinally — the world's longest-running household panel study, originally designed to track entries and exits from poverty. Source PDF →
1972–74
Social Security expansion & SSI created
A 20% across-the-board Social Security increase plus automatic COLAs dramatically reduces elderly poverty. The Supplemental Security Income (SSI) program for aged, blind, and disabled Americans becomes law in 1974, along with EITC in 1975. Source PDF →
1978
OMB formalizes the official poverty measure
Statistical Policy Directive No. 14 locks in the Orshansky-based thresholds as the government's official poverty definition. Critics note it ignores in-kind benefits and regional cost variation. Source PDF →
1964–1981
Rise and fall of the OEO
The Office of Economic Opportunity oversees the bulk of War on Poverty programs for nearly two decades before being dismantled. Its community action approach and evaluation experiments shape future policy research methodology. Source PDF →
1989
National Center for Children in Poverty (NCCP) founded
Columbia University establishes NCCP to focus research on low-income children and families. It later moves to Bank Street College (2019) and becomes part of its Institute for Thriving Children & Families. Source PDF →
1992–95
NAS panel proposes a new poverty measure
The National Academy of Sciences convenes experts who publish Measuring Poverty: A New Approach (1995), arguing the official measure ignores in-kind benefits, payroll taxes, child care costs, and medical expenses. This eventually leads to the Supplemental Poverty Measure. Source PDF →
1996
Welfare reform — PRWORA
The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Act replaces AFDC with TANF, adding work requirements, time limits, and block grants. The shift from cash welfare to work-conditioned support marks a sea change in antipoverty philosophy.
2006
Stanford Center on Poverty and Inequality opens
Stanford launches CPI with support from the Hewlett and Elfenworks foundations, recognizing poverty as "an unprecedented threat" requiring world-class university research. It develops the California Poverty Measure and Pathways Magazine. Source PDF →
2011
Supplemental Poverty Measure & UC Davis center
The Census Bureau publishes its first SPM estimates, incorporating taxes, transfers, and regional housing costs. Separately, UC Davis receives a $4M HHS grant to become one of three federally designated Poverty Research Centers. Mark Rank publishes "Rethinking American Poverty," arguing 60% of Americans will experience poverty between ages 20–75. Source PDF →
2018
Opportunity Insights launches
Raj Chetty, John Friedman, and Nathaniel Hendren found Opportunity Insights at Harvard, using big data and IRS records to map economic mobility by neighborhood. Backed by the Gates Foundation and Chan Zuckerberg Initiative, it partners with cities including Charlotte, Seattle, Minneapolis, and Detroit. Source PDF →
2020s
Pandemic-era expansion & retrenchment
COVID relief temporarily expands the Child Tax Credit, SNAP, and unemployment insurance, cutting child poverty nearly in half in 2021. The Georgetown Center on Poverty and Inequality helps design the monthly CTC payment structure. Expiration of expansions triggers renewed debate on the future of the safety net.
Paradigms

How America Has Studied Poverty

Research approaches have evolved through distinct eras, each layering new tools and frameworks onto earlier ones.

1960s–70s

Income Measurement & Thresholds

Mollie Orshansky's "food budget × 3" formula created the first standardized poverty line. The Census Bureau began annual poverty counts. The focus was on defining who is poor using cash income.

1960s–80s

Experimental & Program Evaluation

The negative income tax experiments and OEO evaluations pioneered randomized social policy research. Head Start, Job Corps, and welfare programs were assessed for causal impact on work, education, and family outcomes.

1968–present

Longitudinal Panel Data

The Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID) showed poverty is dynamic: people cycle in and out. This revolutionized the field, enabling research on intergenerational transmission, neighborhood effects, and life-course risk.

1990s–2010s

Structural & Comparative Analysis

Scholars like Mark Rank reframed poverty as a structural "musical chairs" problem rather than individual failure. Cross-national comparisons (Alesina & Glaeser; Brady) showed U.S. poverty rates reflect policy choices, not cultural differences.

2010s–present

Big Data & Spatial Mobility

Raj Chetty and Opportunity Insights use anonymized IRS records to map mobility at the neighborhood level. Machine learning, administrative data linkages, and the Supplemental Poverty Measure give a far richer picture of material hardship.

So: is research aligned?

Takeaways — what the data on this page actually says

Going back to the central question: how has poverty & inequality research developed, and where does it align—or diverge—from real-world trends and public discourse? Pulling together the OpenAlex trends, the methods/data fingerprint, the Google Books series, and the alignment overlay, three things hold up.

Takeaway 01 · Volume

Research did ramp up as inequality rose

Cited econ + sociology papers tagged poverty or economic inequality grew from a few hundred in the late 1970s to roughly 9,000 per five-year window in 2015–19 (OpenAlex Trends chart).

The top-1% income share and the volume of research broadly co-rise from the late 1990s onward, the period the alignment chart highlights most cleanly.

Takeaway 02 · Subjects

Topics shift—but education, health, and children persist

Research emphases have changed a lot over the decades: neighborhood and place rise with the Chetty–Hendren–Katz wave, global poverty and development economics grow in share, and administrative data unlocks new questions about mobility and policy.

Yet across every five-year window, three themes stay constant: health, education, and children. The field keeps turning over new topics, but these are the durable core of what poverty and inequality research is actually about.

Takeaway 03 · Methods

The field had its own credibility revolution

In the methods fingerprint, “decomposition” was the dominant named technique in the 1980s–90s. By 2015–19 “randomized” / experiment overtakes it, and “longitudinal / panel” passes “survey data” in 2020–24.

The papers Chetty, Hoynes, Hendren, Katz and others produced in that window pushed empirical poverty work toward administrative-record linkage and quasi-experimental designs. It is an exciting time for poverty and inequality research: dedicated labs have multiplied (Opportunity Insights, Columbia CPSP, UC Davis CPIR, and others on the map), and the field is more committed than ever to understanding the mechanisms of poverty and inequality in America.

Verdict

Mostly yes—research has tracked reality, and in places it led public attention. But this is not a one-way story. Real-world levels of poverty and inequality, public discourse, and academic research inform and affect one another: rising inequality drew more scholars in, but new data and methods also opened questions that reshaped the conversation, and landmark books like Piketty’s fed back into both research priorities and public debate.

The rise in cited work is therefore not only about alignment with reality. Richer administrative data, sharper causal methods, and a growing network of dedicated research labs have made the field more empirically productive—and that infrastructure helps explain why publication climbed even where real-world trends and discourse moved more slowly. The honest qualification is that the lag isn’t zero, and the field remains heavily English-language and U.S./Western-European in focus, which the OpenAlex topic mix makes visible.
